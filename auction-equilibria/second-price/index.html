The Second-Price Auction Equilibrium

In a second-price auction, the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid.

In the IPV environment gives the following result:

Proposition: In the second-price auction, for each buyer, truthful bidding, that is, bidding one's true value, is a weakly dominant strategy.

In the interactive demo, adjust the value of the highest bid from other buyers from buyer \(i\)'s perspective. Notice where potential losses arise when you overbid or underbid. Here, values and bids are restricted to be in \([0,100]\), and ties are assumed to be with one other bidder.

\(\underline{v}_i=0\)\(\overline{v}_i=100\)
\(\underline{v}_i=0\)\(\overline{v}_i=100\)

Proof of the proposition

Fix buyer \(i\) and let \(p_i=\max\{x_j \mid j \neq i\}\), where \(x_j\) is buyer \(j\)'s bid. Compare bidding \(v_i\) to some other bid \(x_i\). Suppose first that \(x_i \lt v_i\). Then, there are three cases to consider:

Case 1: \(x_i \lt v_i \leq p_i\). With either bid, buyer \(i\) loses/ties and gets \(0\).

Case 2: \(x_i \leq p_i \lt v_i\). By bidding \(x_i\), buyer \(i\) loses/ties and gets at most \(v_i-p_i\gt 0\), whereas by bidding \(v_i\), he wins and always gets \(v_i-p_i\gt 0\).

Case 3: \(p_i \lt x_i \lt v_i \). With either bid, buyer \(i\) wins and gets \(v_i-p_i\).

If \(x_i \gt v_i\), the reasoning is similar; overbidding only introduces the possibility of winning when buyer \(i\) does not want to, that is, when the second-highest bid is above his value. Thus, truthful bidding is a weakly dominant strategy for each buyer.

Notes

  • Truthful bidding remains a dominant strategy for buyers in the second-price auction under several extensions, such as risk aversion.
