The IPV First-Price Auction Equilibrium

One indivisible object is to be sold in a sealed-bid auction with no reserve price. There are \(n \ge 2\) risk-neutral bidders. Bidders have values \(V_1, \ldots, V_n \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\) \(F(v)\). Each bidder \(i\) privately observes his realized value \(v_i\) hence independent, private values (IPV). We also assume that \(F\) has continuous, strictly positive density \(f\) on a positive support with finite expectation. Next, all bidders simultaneously submit their bids. The highest bidder wins the object, gets his value for the object, and pays his own bid, while losing bidders get nothing and pay zero. Ties are broken uniformly at random though occur with probability zero since \(f\) is continuous.

Consider bidder 1 with value \(v_1\). Let \(Y_1 = \max\{X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}\}\) , where \(X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}F(v)\). Suppose bidders \(2,\ldots,n\) bid according to the symmetric, increasing function

\[ \beta^{\mathrm{I}}(v_i) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_1 \mid v_i > Y_1\right], i = 2, \ldots, n. \]

In the interactive demo, you take the perspective of bidder 1, considering different bids in response to the other bidders using this strategy. Here, \(V_i = a + (b-a)X_i\), where \(X_1, \ldots, X_{n} \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) (\(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 1\) for \(\operatorname{Uniform}[a,b]\) values). Choose your value \(v_1\) or randomly draw it. Then, propose a bid \(x_1\), and observe how changing it affects your expected payoff.

Optimal bid for bidder 1

After playing with the bid slider for a few different parameter settings, you may notice that the best bidder 1 can do, across all possible values he may have, is to follow the same bidding strategy as everyone else. Let us verify this more rigorously.

Let \(G(y)=F(y)^{n-1}\) denote the distribution function of \(Y_1\), and let \(g\) denote its density. First, notice that it is not optimal to bid above \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(b)\), since, by assumption, no opponent will bid above \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(b)\). Similarly, there is no gain from bidding below \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(a)=a\). Because \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}\) is continuous and strictly increasing, every remaining bid can be written as \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(z)\) for some \(z\in[a,b]\). Thus, we can compare bidding \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(v_1)\) with bidding \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(z)\). At \(z=a\), the expressions below are understood by continuity.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\!\left[ u\!\left(v_1,\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(z)\right) \right] &= \underbrace{G(z)}_{\text{probability of winning}} \underbrace{\left(v_1-\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(z)\right)} _{\text{payoff upon winning}} \\ &=G(z)\left(v_1-\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(z)\right) \\ &=G(z)v_1 -G(z)\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_1\mid Y_1\le z\right] \\ &=G(z)v_1-\int_a^z y g(y)\,dy \\ &=G(z)v_1-G(z)z+\int_a^z G(y)\,dy \\ &=G(z)(v_1-z)+\int_a^z G(y)\,dy. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\!\left[ u\!\left(v_1,\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(v_1)\right) \right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[ u\!\left(v_1,\beta^{\mathrm{I}}(z)\right) \right] \\ &\quad = G(v_1)(v_1-v_1)+\int_a^{v_1}G(y)\,dy -\left[G(z)(v_1-z)+\int_a^zG(y)\,dy\right] \\ &\quad = G(z)(z-v_1)+\int_z^{v_1}G(y)\,dy. \end{aligned} \]

Because \(G\) is nondecreasing, this difference is weakly positive for every \(z\in[a,b]\). Thus, given that bidders \(2,\ldots,n\) bid according to \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}\), it is optimal for bidder 1 to also bid according to \(\beta^{\mathrm{I}}\).

Notes

  • A more descriptive name for this setting may be "independent, private values first-price auction with risk-neutral, symmetric bidders, no reserve price, and no budget-constraints," but IPV is a common abbreviation for this setting in the auction-theory literature.
  • We have not shown this equilibrium is unique, but a little more work shows that it is the unique symmetric, increasing equilibrium. See Krishna (2009), sec. 2.3, pp. 14–16. If we do not restrict to symmetric, increasing equilibria, even more work is required.
