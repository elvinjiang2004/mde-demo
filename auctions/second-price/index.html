The Second-Price Auction Equilibrium

One indivisible object is to be sold in a sealed-bid auction with no reserve price. There are \(n \ge 2\) risk-neutral bidders. Bidders have values \(V_1, \ldots, V_n \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\) \(F(v)\). Each bidder \(i\) privately observes his realized value \(v_i\), hence independent, private values (IPV). We also assume that \(F\) has continuous, strictly positive density \(f\) on a positive support with finite expectation. Next, all bidders simultaneously submit their bids. The highest bidder wins the object, gets his value for the object, and pays the highest opposing bid, while losing bidders get nothing and pay zero. Ties are broken uniformly at random though occur with probability zero since \(f\) is continuous.

Consider bidder 1 with value \(v_1\). Suppose bidders \(2,\ldots,n\) bid according to the symmetric, increasing function

\[ \beta^{\mathrm{II}}(v_i)=v_i,\quad i=2,\ldots,n. \]

In the interactive demo, you take the perspective of bidder 1, considering different bids in response to the other bidders using this strategy. Dominant strategy for bidder 1

Notice from the demo where the loss from underbidding and overbidding comes from. Underbidding causes you to lose in cases where you would have won and received a positive payoff. Overbidding causes you to win in cases where you receive a negative payoff and would have preferred to lose. In fact, we can show that it is a weakly dominant strategy to bid truthfully, that is, to bid your own value, in the second-price auction.

Suppose bidders \(2,\ldots,n\) bid \(x_2,\ldots,x_n\). Let \(p_1=\max\{x_i \mid i=2,\ldots,n\}\). Let \(x^-\) denote an alternative underbid, so \(x^- < v_1\), and let \(x^+\) denote an alternative overbid, so \(v_1 < x^+\). We consider four cases for \(p_1\):

Case 1: \(p_1 < x^- < v_1\). With either bid, bidder 1 wins and gets \(v_1-p_1>0\).

Case 2: \(x^- < p_1 < v_1\). By bidding \(x^-\), bidder 1 loses and gets \(0\), whereas by bidding \(v_1\), he wins and gets \(v_1-p_1>0\).

Case 3: \(v_1 < p_1 < x^+\). By bidding \(x^+\), bidder 1 wins and gets \(v_1-p_1<0\), whereas by bidding \(v_1\), he loses and gets \(0\).

Case 4: \(v_1 < x^+ < p_1\). With either bid, bidder 1 loses and gets \(0\).

Through these four cases, bidding \(v_1\) gives at least as good of an outcome as, and sometimes a better outcome than, the underbid or overbid. It is also easy to verify that this is true in the cases where \(p_1=x^-\), \(p_1=v_1\), and \(p_1=x^+\). Then, since the initial underbid and overbid were chosen arbitrarily, this holds for all underbids and overbids, which is just all alternative bids. Of course, there is no special reason we considered bidder 1, so it is easy to extend this reasoning to all bidders.

Notes

  • The dominance of truthful bidding is robust under several extensions, including risk aversion, interdependent values, and asymmetric bidders.
