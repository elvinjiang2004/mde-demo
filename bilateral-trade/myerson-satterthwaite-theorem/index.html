The Myerson-Satterthwaite Theorem

A seller has an object that she may sell to a buyer. The seller has value \(C \sim F_S(c)\) and the buyer has value \(V \sim F_B(v)\). We assume \(F_S\) has continuous, strictly positive density \(f_S\) on its support \([\underline{c},\overline{c}]\), and, likewise, \(F_B\) has continuous, strictly positive density \(f_B\) on its support \([\underline{v},\overline{v}]\). We also assume the intersection of the supports is also an interval, that is, it is not empty and not a single point. The buyer and seller observe their respective values, \(v\) and \(c\), but not the other's.

We want to find a direct bargaining mechanism consisting of an allocation rule and two payment rules \((q(v,c),p_B(v,c),p_S(v,c))\) in this environment that satisfies:

  1. (Bayesian) Incentive Compatibility (IC): Given the seller reports her true value, it is optimal for the buyer to do the same, and vice versa.
  2. (Interim) Individual Rationality (IR): Both the buyer and seller have a weakly positive expected payoff after observing their values.
  3. (Weak, ex-ante) Budget Balance (BB): The trade does not need to be subsidized by an outside party in expectation.
  4. (Ex-post) Pareto Efficiency (PE): The mechanism maximizes welfare at every realized value profile. In other words, the person who values the object most always ends up with it.

The Myerson-Satterthwaite Theorem says that a such a mechanism does not exist. In the interactive demo, design a direct bargaining mechanism and see where it fails. Here, \(V,C \mathrel{\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}} \operatorname{Uniform}[0,1]\).

Incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and the payment rule

You may wonder why you do not need to specify the payment rules in addition to your allocation rule, even though a direct bargaining mechanism consists of all three. Before we can answer this fully, let's specify the following quantities:

For a buyer with value \(v\), given the seller reports truthfully:

  • \(\displaystyle Q_B(v)=\int_{\underline{c}}^{\overline{c}} q(v,c)\,f_S(c)\,dc\), his interim probability of trade;
  • \(\displaystyle P_B(v)=\int_{\underline{c}}^{\overline{c}} p_B(v,c)\,f_S(c)\,dc\), his interim expected payment from the buyer;
  • \(\displaystyle U_B(v)=v\,Q_B(v)-P_B(v)\), his interim expected payoff;
  • \(\displaystyle \phi_B(v)=v-\frac{1-F_B(v)}{f_B(v)}\), his virtual value.

For a seller with value \(c\), given the buyer reports truthfully:

  • \(\displaystyle Q_S(c)=\int_{\underline{v}}^{\overline{v}} q(v,c)\,f_B(v)\,dv\), her interim probability of trade;
  • \(\displaystyle P_S(c)=\int_{\underline{v}}^{\overline{v}} p_S(c,v)\,f_B(v)\,dv\), her interim expected payment to the seller;
  • \(\displaystyle U_S(c)=P_S(c)-c\,Q_S(c)\), her interim expected payoff;
  • \(\displaystyle \phi_S(c)=c+\frac{F_S(c)}{f_S(c)}\), her virtual value.

Now, we can rewrite the IC, IR conditions as

  • IC: \(\displaystyle U_B(v)\ge vQ_B(v')-P_B(v')\), for all \(v'\in[\underline{v},\overline{v}]\), and \(\displaystyle U_S(c)\ge P_S(c')-cQ_S(c')\), for all \(c'\in[\underline{c},\overline{c}]\);
  • IR: \(\displaystyle U_B(v)\ge 0\), for all \(v\in[\underline{v},\overline{v}]\), and \(\displaystyle U_S(c)\ge 0\), for all \(c\in[\underline{c},\overline{c}]\).

We have the following theorem thanks to Börgers, et al. (2015):

Theorem: An allocation rule \(q(v,c)\) is IC and IR implementable by some payment rules if and only if \(Q_B\) is weakly increasing and \(Q_S\) is weakly decreasing.

Proof: Start with the forward implication. Suppose \(q(v,c)\) is IC and IR implementable by some payment rules. Then, for the buyer, suppose \(v_1\le v_2\). By IC, \(\displaystyle = v_2Q_B(v_2)-P_B(v_2)\geq v_2Q_B(v_1)-P_B(v_1). \)

\[ U_B(v_1) = v_1Q_B(v_1)-P_B(v_1)\geq v_1Q_B(v_2)-P_B(v_2) \]
\[ U_B(v_2) = v_2Q_B(v_2)-P_B(v_2)\geq v_2Q_B(v_1)-P_B(v_1). \]

Adding the two inequalities gives

\(\displaystyle v_1Q_B(v_1)-P_B(v_1) + v_2Q_B(v_2)-P_B(v_2)\) \(\displaystyle \geq\) \(\displaystyle v_1Q_B(v_2)-P_B(v_2) + v_2Q_B(v_1)-P_B(v_1)\) \(\displaystyle \iff\) \(\displaystyle v_1Q_B(v_1) + v_2Q_B(v_2)\) \(\displaystyle \geq\) \(\displaystyle v_1Q_B(v_2) + v_2Q_B(v_1)\) \(\displaystyle \iff\) \(\displaystyle v_1Q_B(v_1) + v_2Q_B(v_2) - v_1Q_B(v_2) - v_2Q_B(v_1)\) \(\displaystyle \geq\) \(\displaystyle 0\) \(\displaystyle \iff\) \(\displaystyle v_1(Q_B(v_1)-Q_B(v_2)) - v_2(Q_B(v_1)-Q_B(v_2))\) \(\displaystyle \geq\) \(\displaystyle 0\) \(\displaystyle \iff\) \(\displaystyle (v_1-v_2)(Q_B(v_1)-Q_B(v_2))\) \(\displaystyle \geq\) \(\displaystyle 0\)

Since \((v_1 - v_2)\le 0\), dividing over flips the inequality, giving \(Q_B(v_1)-Q_B(v_2)\le 0\), or \(Q_B(v_1)\le Q_B(v_2)\). A similar argument shows that \(Q_S\) is weakly decreasing.
