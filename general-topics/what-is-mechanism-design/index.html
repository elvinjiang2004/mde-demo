What is Mechanism Design?

If we want people to behave in a certain way, we must construct rules that encourage them to do so. Then, what is the best way to construct these rules? This is broadly what the study of mechanism design aims to answer.

Suppose a king needs to provision food for a famished population. He prefers to give more food to those who are hungrier, and decides that the time since someone has last ate is a good enough proxy for their hunger. Should he just ask people when they last ate, and give more to those who say it has been longer? If he could guarantee everyone responds truthfully, this would be a viable plan. However, a person who anticipates this may lie about his time in order to receive more provisions.

We can see the main obstruction in the previous example: private information. If the king knew exactly when everyone last ate, his problem would be easily solved. Although we can not really remedy the king's situation, some scenarios have more structure that can be manipulated to elicit people's private information. For example, imagine someone has a painting she needs to sell, but she has no idea how much the potential buyers may value it. How should she sell it? A common method is via a sealed-bid, first-price auction where each buyer submits a bid secretly, and the highest bidder wins and pays his bid. We can imagine this as the seller asking each buyer "What is your value for this painting?" and choosing the buyer who reports he has the highest value to win. Notice how players' incentives change in answering the seller's question vs. in answering the king's question. The key difference is that lying too much to the seller is costly while lying any amount to the king is not. Claiming to have a value too low makes a buyer lose too much, despite paying very little. Claiming to have a value too high makes a buyer pay too much, despite winning very often. There is a middle-ground a buyer has to find for his specific value, allowing his reported value/bid to serve as a proxy for his true value.

Private information is the central obstacle in mechanism design. It is safe to say that it is the point of mechanism design in that without it all of the problems in mechanism design become trivial. In addition to overcoming this obstacle, much of mechanism design tries to figure out how to do it in a desirable way. For example, conducting an auction that generates as much revenue as possible for the seller or getting two people to trade an object that generates as much social welfare as possible.

The study of mechanism design, and more generally economic theory, uses rigorous mathematical language, such as from game theory or analysis. This gives two primary advantages over formulating and arguing for these theories using only English, or other informal languages/reasonings. First, it makes the statements (essentially) maximally precise and the reasonings perfectly verifiable. If the theories and their arguments were formulated entirely in English, one would have to, or at least could, question both the meaning of the statements and the validity of the reasonings. Second, which is more of a consequence of the first, is that having such solid foundations and frameworks makes extensions and adaptations to theories particularly easy to formulate.

Economic models are not perfect. When reading the assumptions of a particular model, there are many assumptions one could point out that disagree with reality. For example, that players are risk-neutral, that they have common knowledge of the game's rules, that they can costlessly misrepresent their private information, etc. One may even disagree that players' preferences can be represented by a utility function or that preferences even exist in the first place. However, the best model of economics coincides with the best model of physics in that if and only if we could perfectly model the physical world, could we also perfectly model any economic interactions. Unfortuntely, we, as humans, are far from this perfect model, so we must make our peace with some vast simplifications.
