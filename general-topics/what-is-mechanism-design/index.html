What is Mechanism Design?

If we want people to behave in a certain way, we must construct rules that encourage them to do so. Then, what is the best way to accomplish this? This is broadly what the study of mechanism design aims to answer.

Suppose a king needs to provision food for a famished population. He prefers to give more food to those who are hungrier, and decides that the time since someone has last ate is a good enough proxy for their hunger. Should he just ask people when they last ate, and give more to those who say it has been longer? A person who anticipates this may lie about his time in order to receive more provisions. In this case, without additional structure, the king cannot really achieve his desired outcome.

We can see the main obstruction in the previous example: private information. If the king knew exactly when everyone last ate, his problem would be easily solved. Although we could not remedy the king's situation, some scenarios have more structure that can be manipulated to elicit people's private information. For example, imagine having a painting you need to sell, but you have no idea how much your potential buyers may value it. How should you sell it? A popular method is via a first price auction where everyone submits a bid simultaneously, and the highest bidder wins and pays his bid. We can imagine this as us asking each potential buyer "What is your value for this painting?" and choosing the person who claims he has the highest value to win. Notice how people's incentives change in answering the auction's question vs. in answering the king's question. The key difference is that lying too much to the auction is costly while lying any amount to the king is not. Claiming to have a value too low makes you lose too much, despite paying very little. Claiming to have a value too high makes you pay too much, despite winning very often. There is a perfect middle-ground a bidder has to find for his specific value, allowing his claim/bid to serve as a proxy for his value.

Private information is the central obstacle in mechanism design. It is safe to say that it is the point of mechanism design, in that without it, all of the problems in mechanism design become trivial. In addition to overcoming this obstacle, much of mechanism design tries to figure out how to do it in a desirable way. For example, conducting an auction that generates as much revenue as possible or getting two people to trade an object that generates as much social welfare as possible.
