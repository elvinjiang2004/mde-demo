Notice from the demo where the loss from underbidding and overbidding come from. Underbidding causes you to lose in cases where you would've won and get a positive payoff. Overbidding causes you to win in cases where you get a negative payoff and would've preferred to lose and get zero. In fact, we can show that it is a weakly dominant strategy to bid <em>truthfully</em>, that is, to bid your own value in the second-price auction.
Suppose bidders 2,...,n bid x_2,...,x_n. Let p_1=max{x_i|i=2,...,n}. Let x^- denote an alternative underbid, so x^-<v_1, and let x^+ denote an alternative overbid, so v_1<x^+. We consider four cases for p_1:
Case 1: p_1 < x^-< v_1.
With either bid, bidder 1 wins and gets v_1-p_1>0.
Case 2: x^-< p_1 < v_1.
By bidding x^-, bidder 1 loses and gets 0, whereas by bidding v_1, he wins and gets v_1-p_1>0.
Case 3: v_1 < p_1 < x^+.
By bidding x^+, bidder 1 wins and gets v_1-p_1<0, whereas by bidding v_1, he loses and gets 0.
Case 4: v_1 < x^+ < p_1
With either bid, bidder 1 loses and gets 0.
Through these four cases, bidding v_1 gives at least as good of an outcome, and sometimes a better outcome, than the underbid or overbid. It is also easy to verify that this is true in the cases where p_1=x^-, p_1=v_1, and p_1 = x^+. Then, since the initial underbid and overbid were chosen arbitrarily, this holds for all underbids and overbids, which is just all alternative bids.
